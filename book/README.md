When people speak about global warming, the explanation usually begins with CO₂.

Carbon dioxide increases in the atmosphere.
Greenhouse gases trap heat.
The global average temperature rises.
Therefore, humanity must reduce emissions.

This explanation is not wrong.

CO₂ is an important greenhouse gas.
The burning of fossil fuels has increased atmospheric CO₂ concentration.
The increase in greenhouse gases has changed the Earth’s radiative balance.
Reducing emissions is necessary.

This book does not deny that.

However, there is one question that has not been asked seriously enough.

Is global warming really only a CO₂ problem?

If we focus only on CO₂ concentration, we may miss the larger causal structure behind global warming.

Forests have been lost.
Soils have been degraded.
Water cycles have been disrupted.
Wetlands, rivers, groundwater systems, and local cooling functions have weakened.
Cities have become heat-retaining structures.
Organic matter no longer returns to the soil in the way it once did.
Soil microorganisms have declined in many places.
Ocean surfaces have accumulated heat.
Vertical mixing and ocean circulation have weakened.
Coastal ecosystems have been damaged by nutrient imbalance, eutrophication, red tides, oxygen depletion, and dead zones.
Phytoplankton and marine ecosystems, which support carbon fixation and oxygen production, are under pressure.

These are not separate issues.

They are connected.

The Earth originally had natural systems that absorbed, fixed, dispersed, circulated, and released both carbon and heat.

Forests cooled the land through shade and transpiration.
Soils stored carbon and water through organic matter and microorganisms.
Wetlands and rivers stabilized water movement.
The ocean absorbed heat and carbon.
Phytoplankton supported marine carbon cycles.
Evaporation, condensation, clouds, rainfall, groundwater, rivers, and ocean circulation moved heat across the planet.

In other words, the Earth was not simply a passive object being warmed by CO₂.

The Earth had cooling functions.

The Earth had circulation functions.

The Earth had carbon-fixing functions.

The Earth had biological and physical systems that prevented heat and carbon from accumulating too easily at the surface.

But humanity has weakened many of these systems.

This is why global warming should not be understood only as an increase in CO₂ concentration.

Global warming is also the result of the weakening and loss of the Earth’s natural cooling functions.

It is the result of damaged forests, damaged soils, disrupted water cycles, weakened ocean circulation, degraded ecosystems, and broken organic matter circulation.

This book presents the following central idea:

CO₂ is a cause.
But CO₂ is also a result.

CO₂ is a cause because fossil fuel combustion directly increases atmospheric carbon dioxide and strengthens the greenhouse effect.

But CO₂ is also a result because the natural systems that once absorbed, fixed, and circulated carbon have been weakened.

When forests disappear, less carbon is fixed.
When soils degrade, less carbon is stored.
When organic matter circulation collapses, soil life weakens.
When water cycles are disrupted, natural cooling declines.
When ocean circulation and marine ecosystems weaken, the ocean’s ability to absorb and cycle carbon is affected.

As a result, carbon and heat that should have been absorbed, fixed, dispersed, or circulated remain more easily in the atmosphere, on the land surface, and in the ocean surface layer.

This is the deeper causal structure of global warming.

It is not enough to ask only:

How much CO₂ did we emit?

We must also ask:

How much natural cooling capacity have we lost?
How much carbon-fixing capacity have we destroyed?
How much water-cycle stability has disappeared?
How much soil life has been weakened?
How much ocean circulation has been damaged?
How much heat can the Earth still disperse naturally?

The standard climate discussion often focuses on emissions, carbon accounting, carbon neutrality, and carbon credits.

These are important.

Did we actually cool the Earth?

Soil is not only dirt.

The standard explanation of global warming is simple.

Humanity burns fossil fuels.
Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases increase in the atmosphere.
These gases trap more heat.
The global average temperature rises.

This explanation is widely accepted, and it is scientifically important.

CO₂ does absorb infrared radiation.
Greenhouse gases do affect the Earth’s energy balance.
Fossil fuel combustion has increased atmospheric CO₂ concentration.
Human activity has clearly changed the climate system.

For this reason, reducing greenhouse gas emissions is necessary.

There is no serious climate solution that can ignore emission reduction.

However, there is a problem when this explanation becomes too narrow.

When global warming is described only as a CO₂ problem, many other causal factors disappear from view.

The loss of forests disappears.
The weakening of transpiration disappears.
The decline of soil microorganisms disappears.
The collapse of organic matter circulation disappears.
The loss of water retention in soils disappears.
The drying of landscapes disappears.
The heat-retaining structure of cities disappears.
The degradation of wetlands, rivers, and groundwater systems disappears.
The accumulation of ocean heat disappears.
The weakening of vertical ocean mixing disappears.
The pressure on phytoplankton and marine ecosystems disappears.
The expansion of red tides, oxygen depletion, and dead zones disappears.

These are not secondary issues.

They are part of the causal structure of global warming.

The Earth is not warmed only because more heat is trapped.
The Earth is also warming because many of the systems that once absorbed, moved, stored, and released heat have been weakened.

A planet does not remain cool only by having a stable atmosphere.

It remains cool through circulation.

It remains cool through forests.
It remains cool through water.
It remains cool through soil.
It remains cool through oceans.
It remains cool through evaporation and condensation.
It remains cool through clouds and rainfall.
It remains cool through living ecosystems.

This is the limit of the standard explanation.

It explains the greenhouse effect.
But it does not fully explain the loss of the Earth’s natural cooling functions.

The Greenhouse Effect Is Real, But It Is Not the Whole System

The greenhouse effect is often explained as follows.

Sunlight reaches the Earth.
The land and ocean absorb part of that energy.
The Earth emits heat back toward space as infrared radiation.
Greenhouse gases absorb some of that outgoing radiation and re-emit it, keeping more heat within the climate system.

This is a real and important process.

Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth would be too cold for current life as we know it.
But when greenhouse gases increase too much, the balance changes.

This is the foundation of modern climate science.

However, the Earth’s temperature is not determined by greenhouse gases alone.

It is also affected by cloud formation, water vapor movement, ocean currents, snow and ice, vegetation, soil moisture, land use, albedo, aerosols, evaporation, transpiration, and heat exchange between the atmosphere, land, and ocean.

The climate system is not a single-variable machine.

It is a complex circulation system.

When forests are removed, the land reflects, absorbs, stores, and releases heat differently.
When soils dry, evaporation and transpiration decline.
When wetlands are destroyed, local cooling and water retention weaken.
When cities expand with asphalt, concrete, glass, and air-conditioning exhaust, heat is stored and released differently.
When ocean surfaces warm, stratification can increase, reducing vertical mixing and nutrient circulation.

All of these changes affect the movement of heat.

Therefore, a climate explanation that focuses only on CO₂ is incomplete.

It may be correct within its own narrow frame.
But it does not describe the whole planetary system.

Forests Are Not Only Carbon Stores

In many climate discussions, forests are treated mainly as carbon sinks.

This is understandable.

Trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis.
They store carbon in trunks, branches, leaves, roots, and soils.
Deforestation releases carbon and reduces future carbon absorption.

But forests are more than carbon stores.

Forests are cooling systems.

They shade the land surface.
They hold moisture.
They slow runoff.
They support soil organisms.
They help form clouds.
They release water vapor through transpiration.
They connect groundwater, rivers, rainfall, and atmosphere.

Transpiration is especially important.

When plants release water vapor from their leaves, heat is used to turn liquid water into vapor.
This process removes heat from the local environment.
It is similar to the way sweat cools the human body.

A healthy forest is therefore not just absorbing carbon.

It is pumping water into the atmosphere.
It is cooling the land surface.
It is helping maintain rainfall.
It is stabilizing local and regional climates.

When forests disappear, this cooling function weakens.

The result is not only more CO₂.
The result is also less water vapor circulation, less shade, less soil moisture, less cloud formation, and more heat accumulation.

This is why deforestation must not be understood only as the loss of carbon storage.

It is also the loss of a planetary cooling organ.

Soil Is Not Only Ground

Soil is often treated as something passive.

But healthy soil is alive.

It contains microorganisms, fungi, plant roots, insects, minerals, water, air, and organic matter.
These elements form a living structure that stores carbon, retains water, supports plants, and stabilizes ecosystems.

Healthy soil can absorb rainwater.
It can store moisture.
It can reduce runoff.
It can support vegetation.
It can help maintain transpiration.
It can store carbon as soil organic matter.

Damaged soil behaves differently.

When soil loses organic matter, its structure weakens.
When microorganisms decline, nutrient cycling becomes poorer.
When soil becomes compacted or exposed, water runs off more quickly.
When soil dries, vegetation weakens.
When vegetation weakens, transpiration declines.
When transpiration declines, local cooling declines.

This is not only an agricultural issue.

It is a climate issue.

The loss of healthy soil means the loss of a hidden cooling system.

Soil regeneration is therefore climate action.

Returning organic matter to the soil, protecting microorganisms, reducing unnecessary chemical stress, restoring plant diversity, and rebuilding water retention are not just farming techniques.

They are part of restoring the Earth’s ability to cool itself.

Water Is Not Only a Resource

Water is often discussed as a resource.

Drinking water.
Irrigation water.
Industrial water.
Hydropower.
Flood control.

But in the climate system, water is also a heat-transfer mechanism.

Water absorbs heat.
Water evaporates.
Water vapor moves through the atmosphere.
Water condenses into clouds.
Rain falls.
Water enters rivers, soils, wetlands, groundwater, and oceans.
Ocean currents move heat across the planet.

Through phase change, water moves enormous amounts of energy.

Evaporation removes heat from the surface.
Condensation releases heat in the atmosphere.
Clouds reflect sunlight and influence radiation.
Rainfall redistributes water and heat.

This means that the water cycle is one of the Earth’s natural cooling and heat-distribution systems.

When landscapes dry, water-cycle cooling weakens.

When forests are removed, transpiration weakens.
When soils lose organic matter, water retention weakens.
When wetlands are destroyed, water storage weakens.
When cities seal the ground with concrete and asphalt, infiltration weakens.
When rainwater is quickly drained away, local cooling weakens.

A dry landscape becomes a heating surface.

A wet, living landscape becomes a cooling system.

This is one of the most important differences that carbon accounting often fails to capture.

Carbon Accounting Is Not the Same as Cooling

Carbon accounting has become a major part of climate policy.

Companies, governments, and institutions measure emissions.
They set reduction targets.
They purchase offsets.
They use carbon credits.
They aim for carbon neutrality or net zero.

These systems can play a role.

But carbon accounting has a major limitation.

It measures carbon.

It does not necessarily measure cooling.

A project may reduce or offset a certain amount of CO₂ on paper.
But that does not automatically mean the local environment has cooled.
It does not automatically mean water cycles have recovered.
It does not automatically mean soil life has returned.
It does not automatically mean forests are functioning as living cooling systems.
It does not automatically mean ocean circulation has improved.
It does not automatically mean cities have reduced their heat load.

This is the gap between carbon accounting and planetary cooling.

If climate policy rewards only carbon numbers, then many real cooling actions remain undervalued.

For example, restoring soil organic matter may improve water retention and reduce local heat stress.
Rebuilding urban water systems may reduce heat-island effects.
Restoring wetlands may stabilize water cycles.
Regenerating forests may improve transpiration and cloud formation.
Supporting coastal ecosystems may improve marine productivity and carbon cycling.

These actions may not always fit easily into carbon-credit accounting.

But they can contribute to cooling.

This is why a new framework is needed.

Climate policy should not abandon carbon accounting.
But it must expand beyond it.

It must include cooling accounting.

It must evaluate the recovery of natural cooling functions.

It must measure not only how much carbon is reduced, but also how much heat load is lowered, how much water circulation is restored, how much soil and forest function has recovered, and how much resilience has been rebuilt.

This is the basic idea behind Cooling Credits.

The Paris Agreement was adopted in 2015.

It became a symbolic turning point in global climate policy.

The world agreed to hold the increase in global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, and to pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C.

This was an important moment.

For the first time, almost the entire international community shared a common climate target.

Governments began to set emission-reduction goals.
Companies began to announce net-zero commitments.
Carbon neutrality became a common phrase.
Renewable energy expanded.
Electric vehicles became more visible.
Carbon credits and offset markets grew.
Environmental, social, and governance investment became a major theme.

On the surface, climate action seemed to be moving forward.

But there is one question that must be asked.

After the decade that followed the Paris Agreement, did the Earth become cooler?

The answer is not encouraging.

The world talked more about climate change.
The world measured more emissions.
The world created more carbon markets.
The world announced more net-zero targets.

But the climate system continued to warm.
Extreme heat events intensified.
Ocean heat accumulated.
Wildfires became larger and more destructive in many regions.
Droughts and floods became more severe.
Coral reefs experienced repeated bleaching.
Ice continued to decline.
The gap between climate targets and actual planetary conditions became harder to ignore.

This does not mean that all climate action failed.

It means that climate action was too narrow.

The decade after the Paris Agreement was not a decade of doing nothing.

It was a decade of doing something important, but incomplete.

The world focused heavily on emissions.
It focused heavily on carbon accounting.
It focused heavily on carbon credits, offsets, targets, and net-zero statements.

But it did not sufficiently focus on the Earth’s natural cooling functions.

It did not sufficiently focus on forests as cooling systems.
It did not sufficiently focus on soil microorganisms and organic matter circulation.
It did not sufficiently focus on water-cycle restoration.
It did not sufficiently focus on wetlands, rivers, groundwater, and transpiration.
It did not sufficiently focus on ocean circulation, oxygen supply, phytoplankton, and marine ecosystems.
It did not sufficiently focus on cities as heat-retaining structures.
It did not sufficiently ask whether climate action was actually reducing heat load.

This is the bias of climate policy.

It became carbon-centered.

CO₂ is a major greenhouse gas.
Human emissions increase atmospheric CO₂.
Therefore, reduce emissions.
If emissions cannot be fully reduced immediately, offset them.
If emissions and offsets balance, call it carbon neutral or net zero.

This logic is simple.
It is measurable.
It can be turned into laws, corporate targets, financial products, credits, and markets.

That is why it spread so quickly.

But simplicity can also hide complexity.

The Earth’s climate system is not a balance sheet.

A company may purchase carbon credits.
A country may announce a net-zero target.
A project may claim avoided emissions.
A forest project may estimate carbon absorption.

But the atmosphere, forests, soils, oceans, rivers, clouds, and cities do not respond to accounting language.

They respond to physical and biological reality.

They respond to whether forests are alive.
They respond to whether soil can hold water.
They respond to whether organic matter returns to the land.
They respond to whether vegetation can transpire.
They respond to whether ocean circulation can bring nutrients and oxygen where they are needed.
They respond to whether cities absorb and release too much heat.
They respond to whether ecosystems are strong enough to maintain cooling and carbon cycles.

This is the problem.

Carbon-centered policy may reduce emissions on paper while leaving the Earth’s cooling systems damaged.

It may fund carbon offsets while ignoring water-cycle recovery.
It may value forests mainly as carbon storage while underestimating transpiration and local cooling.
It may count avoided emissions without measuring whether heat load has actually decreased.
It may treat climate action as a numerical correction instead of a planetary repair project.

This is not enough.

The Problem with Treating 2°C as a Safe Line

The Paris Agreement focused on keeping warming well below 2°C and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.

These targets are politically and scientifically important.

But there is a danger in how people interpret them.

Some people hear 2°C and imagine a safe boundary.

They imagine that below 2°C, the system is still manageable, and above 2°C, danger begins.

But climate damage does not begin suddenly at 2°C.

Damage is already visible before that.

Coral bleaching does not wait for 2°C.
Wildfire risk does not wait for 2°C.
Drought stress does not wait for 2°C.
Soil degradation does not wait for 2°C.
Ocean heat accumulation does not wait for 2°C.
Ecosystem decline does not wait for 2°C.
Extreme rainfall does not wait for 2°C.
Glacier loss does not wait for 2°C.

Climate thresholds are not like a door that remains closed until one exact number is reached.

They are more like stress points in a living system.

As warming increases, pressure accumulates.
As pressure accumulates, weak points begin to fail.
As weak points fail, feedback loops begin to strengthen.

A forest weakened by drought burns more easily.
A burned forest stores less carbon and releases more heat.
Degraded soil holds less water.
Dry soil supports less vegetation.
Less vegetation means less transpiration.
Less transpiration means less cooling and less moisture recycling.
Warmer oceans stratify more easily.
Stratified oceans mix less effectively.
Less mixing can reduce nutrient supply.
Reduced nutrient supply affects phytoplankton.
Damaged marine ecosystems weaken carbon cycling.

These are feedbacks.

They do not wait politely for political targets.

This is why 2°C should not be treated as a safe zone.

It should be treated as a warning boundary.

And 1.5°C should not be treated as a symbolic number.

It should be understood as a sign that the Earth system is already under serious stress.

Emissions can be counted.
Credits can be issued.
Offsets can be bought.
Targets can be announced.
Percentages can be reported.
Certificates can be traded.

This creates the appearance of control.

But the planet is not controlled by spreadsheets.

The planet is controlled by flows.

Carbon flows.
Water flows.
Heat flows.
Nutrients flow.
Air flows.
Ocean currents flow.
Organic matter flows.
Energy flows through ecosystems.

When those flows are healthy, the planet has resilience.

When those flows are broken, the planet becomes fragile.

Carbon accounting may measure one part of one flow.
But it does not automatically measure the whole system.

For example, a forest plantation may count as carbon storage.
But if it is a single-species plantation with poor soil life, low biodiversity, weak water retention, and low ecological resilience, its cooling function may be much weaker than that of a diverse natural forest.

A carbon offset may claim avoided emissions.
But if it does not restore local water cycles, reduce heat load, or improve ecosystem function, its contribution to planetary cooling may be limited.

A net-zero statement may look impressive.
But if it depends heavily on offsets while actual fossil fuel infrastructure remains in place, the physical effect may be weaker than the accounting effect.

This is not a rejection of measurement.

Measurement is necessary.

But the wrong measurement can mislead society.

If we measure only carbon, we may ignore cooling.

If we measure only emissions, we may ignore circulation.

If we measure only credits, we may ignore ecosystems.

If we measure only targets, we may ignore whether the Earth is actually recovering.

Carbon Credits and Their Limits

Carbon credits were created to help reduce or compensate for greenhouse gas emissions.

In theory, they can direct money toward emission-reduction projects, forest protection, renewable energy, methane reduction, and other climate-related activities.

This can be useful.

However, carbon credits also have serious limitations.

First, they can create distance between the emitter and the damage.

A company may continue emitting while purchasing credits elsewhere.
This may reduce emissions on paper, but it does not necessarily change the physical structure of the emitting system.

Second, carbon credits can become difficult to verify.

Was the reduction additional?
Would it have happened anyway?
Is the carbon storage permanent?
Could the forest burn later?
Is the same reduction counted more than once?
Is the project causing damage elsewhere?

These questions are not minor details.

They determine whether the credit represents real climate benefit.

Third, carbon credits often focus on carbon rather than cooling.

They may not adequately evaluate water-cycle recovery, soil regeneration, transpiration, local temperature reduction, biodiversity, ocean function, or resilience.

Fourth, carbon credits can become a permission structure.

If society treats credits as a way to continue emissions without changing the underlying system, then credits become a form of moral escape.

They become a way to say:

We paid, so we are neutral.

But the atmosphere does not care about moral language.

The soil does not care about certificates.

The ocean does not care about accounting claims.

The climate system responds only to real physical change.

For this reason, carbon credits should not be treated as the final solution.

They may have a role.
But they are not enough.

The Missing Value of Natural Cooling

One reason climate policy became carbon-centered is that carbon is relatively easy to count.

CO₂ can be measured in tons.
Emissions can be estimated.

When water evaporates, it absorbs heat.

It produces transpiration.

Transpiration: The Forest’s Cooling Breath

Transpiration is the process by which plants release water vapor through tiny openings in their leaves.

This may sound like a small biological detail.

But it is one of the most important cooling processes on land.

When liquid water becomes vapor, energy is required.
That energy is taken from the surrounding environment.
As a result, the surface cools.

This is the same basic principle by which sweating cools the human body.

When sweat evaporates from skin, it carries heat away.

Plants do something similar for landscapes.

A tree draws water from the soil.
It moves that water through its trunk and branches.
It releases water vapor from its leaves.
The process consumes heat and cools the surrounding air.

A forest with millions or billions of leaves is therefore a vast cooling surface.

It is not cooling through electricity.
It is not cooling through machines.
It is cooling through life and water.

This cooling is difficult to see directly.

A ton of CO₂ can be counted.
A solar panel can be measured.
A factory can report emissions.
A carbon credit can be issued.

But the cooling effect of transpiration is harder to convert into a simple market number.

Because it is harder to count, it is often undervalued.

This is a serious problem.

The Earth has been using transpiration as a natural cooling function long before human technology existed.

If climate policy ignores transpiration, it ignores one of the planet’s most important cooling mechanisms.

The ocean is the largest heat reservoir on Earth.

When people think about global warming, they often imagine the air becoming warmer.

The Ocean as a Heat Reservoir

The atmosphere warms quickly.

The ocean changes more slowly.

This is because water has a high heat capacity.
It can absorb and store large amounts of heat.

For decades, the ocean has taken up most of the excess heat in the climate system.

This has delayed some atmospheric warming.
But it has also created a serious problem.

The heat has not disappeared.

It has entered the ocean.

Ocean heat accumulation affects marine ecosystems, sea levels, weather patterns, storms, coral reefs, ice, and circulation.

A warmer ocean can fuel stronger storms.
It can increase marine heat waves.
It can contribute to coral bleaching.
It can affect fish migration.
It can accelerate ice melt.
It can change the structure of marine ecosystems.
It can alter the exchange of heat and moisture between ocean and atmosphere.

The ocean has protected humanity by absorbing heat.

But this protection has a limit.

If the ocean continues to accumulate heat, its ability to stabilize the climate may weaken.

The ocean is not an infinite sink.

It is a living and physical system under stress.

Surface Warming and Stratification

One major concern is stratification.

Stratification means that layers of water become more separated.

Warm water is lighter than cold water.
When surface water becomes warmer, it tends to stay near the surface.
Colder, denser water remains below.

If the difference between surface and deeper water becomes stronger, vertical mixing can weaken.

Vertical mixing is important because it allows heat, nutrients, oxygen, and carbon to move between layers.

When mixing weakens, the surface ocean can become more isolated from deeper waters.

This can create several problems.

Nutrients from deeper waters may not reach the surface as effectively.
Oxygen may not be distributed as effectively.
Heat can remain concentrated near the surface.
Marine ecosystems may become less stable.
Carbon cycling may be affected.

In simple terms, the ocean’s breathing becomes weaker.

The surface may become warm and nutrient-poor.
The deeper layers may become less connected to the life-rich upper ocean.
The exchange between surface and depth becomes less dynamic.

This matters because the ocean’s climate function depends on circulation.

If circulation weakens, the ocean cannot perform its stabilizing role in the same way.

Vertical Mixing: The Ocean’s Breathing

Vertical mixing can be thought of as part of the ocean’s breathing.

The ocean is not static.

Water moves horizontally through currents.
Water moves vertically through mixing, upwelling, sinking, turbulence, wind-driven processes, and density differences.

These movements distribute heat, oxygen, carbon, and nutrients.

In many regions, nutrients are more abundant in deeper waters.
When deeper water rises toward the surface, it can support phytoplankton growth.
Phytoplankton form the base of many marine food webs.
They absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis.
They produce oxygen.
They support fish, shellfish, zooplankton, and larger marine life.

If vertical mixing or upwelling weakens, the surface layer may receive fewer nutrients.

This can weaken biological productivity.

At the same time, if the surface ocean becomes too warm, some marine organisms experience heat stress.
Coral reefs can bleach.
Fish may migrate.
Plankton communities may shift.
Oxygen levels can decline in some areas.

The ocean’s breathing is therefore not only physical.

It is biological.

It is the connection between water movement and life.

A healthy ocean circulates.
A healthy ocean mixes.
A healthy ocean brings nutrients upward.
A healthy ocean carries oxygen and carbon through its layers.
A healthy ocean supports phytoplankton and food webs.

A weakened ocean becomes more stratified, stressed, and less productive in key regions.

Phytoplankton: The Ocean’s Invisible Forest

Phytoplankton are microscopic organisms that perform photosynthesis in the ocean.

They are small, but their planetary role is enormous.

They absorb CO₂.
They produce oxygen.
They form the base of marine food webs.
They support fish, shellfish, whales, and countless marine organisms.
They help move carbon through the ocean through biological processes.

In this sense, phytoplankton can be called the ocean’s invisible forest.

Forests on land absorb CO₂ and release oxygen.
Phytoplankton do something similar in the ocean.

But unlike forests, phytoplankton are not visible to ordinary human eyes.

Because they are invisible, their importance is often underestimated.

If forests decline, people can see the loss.
If soil dries, people can see cracks.
If rivers flood, people can see damage.
But changes in phytoplankton communities may remain hidden until marine ecosystems are already stressed.

Phytoplankton depend on light, nutrients, temperature, mixing, and ecological balance.

If surface waters become too warm or too stratified, nutrient supply can be affected.
If coastal waters receive too much nutrient pollution, harmful algal blooms can occur.
If ocean chemistry changes, plankton communities may shift.
If ecosystems are disrupted, the entire marine food web can be affected.

This matters for climate.

A weakened phytoplankton system means a weakened marine carbon cycle.

The ocean’s carbon function is not only chemistry.

It is life.

The Biological Pump

One important process in the ocean is often called the biological pump.

Phytoplankton absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis.
Some of that carbon moves through the food web.
Some sinks as dead organisms, waste, or organic particles.
Some carbon reaches deeper waters.
Some may remain stored for long periods.

This process helps move carbon from the surface ocean into deeper layers.

It is not the only ocean carbon process, but it is important.

If phytoplankton communities weaken, shift, or become unstable, the biological pump may also be affected.

This connects ocean life with atmospheric CO₂.

Again, CO₂ is not only an atmospheric issue.

It is connected to ocean biology.

If marine ecosystems weaken, the ocean’s ability to process carbon may weaken.

If the ocean’s ability to process carbon weakens, more CO₂ can remain in the atmosphere or surface ocean.

This is another reason why CO₂ can be both cause and result.

CO₂ increases because of human emissions.
But CO₂ can also be amplified when natural carbon-fixing systems weaken.

Phytoplankton are one of those systems.

Oxygen and the Ocean

Oxygen is another key factor.

Marine life needs oxygen.
Fish, shellfish, crustaceans, and many microorganisms depend on dissolved oxygen in seawater.

Oxygen enters the ocean from the atmosphere and from photosynthesis by marine organisms such as phytoplankton and seaweeds.
It is distributed by mixing and circulation.
It is consumed by respiration and decomposition.

When water becomes warmer, it holds less dissolved oxygen.
When stratification increases, oxygen from the surface may not mix downward as effectively.
When excessive nutrients cause algal blooms, the decomposition of dead algae can consume oxygen.
When oxygen levels fall too low, marine life becomes stressed or dies.

This can lead to hypoxia.

In severe cases, dead zones form.

A dead zone is an area where oxygen is too low to support many forms of marine life.

Dead zones are especially common in coastal regions affected by nutrient runoff.

This connects land and ocean again.

Fertilizers, sewage, soil erosion, and nutrient imbalance can travel through rivers into coastal waters.
There, they can trigger algal blooms.
When the algae die, decomposition consumes oxygen.
Oxygen declines.
Fish and shellfish may leave or die.
The ecosystem weakens.

This is not only a marine pollution issue.

It is part of the climate system.

A low-oxygen ocean is a stressed ocean.
A stressed ocean is less able to function as a stable carbon, heat, oxygen, and life-support system.

The Difference Between Healthy Productivity and Harmful Blooms

It is important to distinguish between healthy marine productivity and harmful algal blooms.

Phytoplankton are necessary.
They support marine life.
They absorb carbon.
They produce oxygen.
They form the foundation of ocean food webs.

But not all algal growth is healthy.

When nutrient flows become excessive and unbalanced, certain algae may grow too rapidly.
This can create harmful blooms.
Some blooms produce toxins.
Some block light.
Some disrupt ecosystems.
When large amounts of algae die and decompose, oxygen is consumed.
This can lead to hypoxia and dead zones.

The problem is not simply that nutrients enter the sea.

In a healthy land-to-sea system, nutrients are delivered gradually and in balance.

The problem is that modern land use often produces sudden, excessive, and distorted nutrient flows.

Deforestation, soil degradation, fertilizer runoff, sewage, and industrial pollution can change the quality and timing of nutrients reaching the ocean.

This can turn a life-supporting flow into a destructive flow.

The ocean needs nutrients.

But it needs the right nutrients, in the right balance, at the right time, through healthy ecological pathways.

This is why soil, agriculture, rivers, and ocean health must be understood together.

The sea cannot be restored if the land remains broken.

Ocean Heat Waves and Coral Bleaching

Ocean heat waves are periods when ocean temperatures become unusually high for a sustained time.

They can cause severe stress to marine ecosystems.

Coral reefs are especially vulnerable.

Corals live in partnership with symbiotic algae that help provide them with energy.
When water becomes too warm, this relationship can break down.
Corals expel the algae and turn white.
This is called coral bleaching.

If heat stress continues, corals may die.

Coral reefs support enormous biodiversity.
They protect coastlines.
They support fisheries and tourism.
They form complex habitats for many marine organisms.

When coral reefs decline, the loss is ecological, economic, and climate-related.

The decline of coral reefs is one of the clearest signs that ocean warming is already damaging the living systems of the sea.

This is another reason why climate action cannot be limited to atmospheric CO₂.

The ocean’s response matters.

A carbon target does not automatically protect coral reefs.
A carbon credit does not automatically reduce ocean heat.
A net-zero pledge does not automatically restore marine ecosystems.

Climate action must include ocean heat, ocean circulation, oxygen, and marine life.

Ocean Acidification

CO₂ also affects the ocean through acidification.

When CO₂ dissolves in seawater, it changes ocean chemistry.
This can reduce the availability of carbonate ions that many marine organisms need to build shells and skeletons.

Organisms such as corals, shellfish, and some plankton can be affected.

Ocean acidification is directly connected to atmospheric CO₂.

This is one reason emission reduction remains essential.

But acidification interacts with other stresses.

A marine organism facing acidification may also face warming, oxygen decline, pollution, habitat damage, overfishing, and nutrient imbalance.

The ocean is being pressured from multiple directions.

This means the solution must also be multi-layered.

Reducing CO₂ emissions is necessary to reduce acidification pressure.
Restoring coastal ecosystems can improve resilience.
Reducing nutrient pollution can reduce dead zones.
Protecting marine habitats can support biodiversity.
Supporting healthy circulation can help maintain oxygen and nutrient balance.
Reducing ocean heat load where possible can protect vulnerable ecosystems.

A carbon-only framework cannot capture all of this.

Modern civilization affects the ocean in many ways.

The Role of Ocean Circulation in Climate

Ocean circulation moves heat around the planet.

Warm water flows from the tropics toward higher latitudes.
Cold water moves through deeper layers.
Currents influence weather, rainfall, storm tracks, marine ecosystems, and regional climates.

Major circulation systems are driven by wind, temperature, salinity, Earth’s rotation, and density differences.

If circulation changes, climate patterns can change.

Some circulation changes are natural.
But human-driven warming may affect circulation by altering temperature, ice melt, freshwater input, and stratification.

This is a serious concern because circulation is one of the ways the Earth distributes heat.

If heat distribution changes, some regions may warm faster.
Some ecosystems may shift.
Some weather patterns may become more unstable.
Some marine food webs may be disrupted.

Again, the climate system is circulation.

It is not just a blanket of greenhouse gases.

Greenhouse gases matter.
But circulation determines how heat, water, carbon, oxygen, and nutrients move through the planet.

This is why the ocean must be central to climate strategy.

Direct Ocean Cooling and Its Risks

Because ocean heat is a major problem, some people may ask whether the ocean can be directly cooled.

This question must be approached carefully.

The ocean is complex.

Moving water, adding nutrients, altering circulation, or changing surface conditions can affect ecosystems.

Therefore, any direct ocean cooling concept must be cautious, small-scale, monitored, reversible, and regionally appropriate.

It must not become an excuse to continue emissions.
It must not replace nature restoration.
It must not ignore ecological risks.
It must not be used as a reckless geoengineering shortcut.

However, carefully designed support for ocean circulation and cooling may become necessary in some contexts.

This may include local or regional measures aimed at supporting vertical mixing, reducing heat stress, improving oxygen conditions, or protecting vulnerable ecosystems.

The purpose should not be to dominate the ocean.

The purpose should be to support the ocean’s ability to breathe.

Any such intervention must follow strict principles:

Low flow.
Intermittent operation.
Wide dispersion.
Continuous monitoring.
Clear stopping conditions.
Ecological safety.
Regional adaptation.
No overclaiming.
No replacement of emission reduction.
No damage to existing marine ecosystems.

The ocean must be treated with humility.

It is not a machine to be forced.

It is a living circulation system to be supported.

It must be evaluated through real environmental outcomes, not only theoretical claims.

This is why ocean-related Cooling Credits, if ever developed, must be strict.

They must not reward reckless intervention.

They must reward careful restoration or support of natural function.

The Definition of Cooling Credits

In this framework, Cooling Credits can be defined as follows.

Cooling Credits are a proposed evaluation framework for measuring, reporting, verifying, and recognizing actions that reduce heat load, restore water cycles, regenerate soils and forests, support ocean circulation, recover natural cooling functions, or contribute to direct cooling in a scientifically and ecologically responsible way.

They are not meant to replace carbon credits.

They are meant to complement them.

Carbon credits focus on greenhouse gas reduction or removal.

Cooling Credits focus on the recovery of cooling capacity.

The difference is important.

A climate project may reduce carbon but have little cooling effect.
A climate project may restore cooling but not fit easily into carbon accounting.
A truly strong project should ideally do both.

For example:

A diverse forest restoration project can store carbon and restore transpiration.
A soil regeneration project can store carbon and improve water retention.
A wetland restoration project can store carbon and stabilize local water cycles.
An urban water-cooling project can reduce heat load and reduce energy demand.
A coastal ecosystem project can support carbon cycling and marine resilience.

Cooling Credits help identify and value the second dimension.

They ask not only:

How much carbon?

But also:

How much cooling?

What Cooling Credits Should Measure

A Cooling Credit framework should measure multiple categories of cooling contribution.

The first category is heat-load reduction.

This includes reductions in land surface temperature, urban heat, stored heat in artificial materials, waste heat, and localized heat stress.

Examples include urban greening, shade creation, water-based cooling, reflective surfaces, reduced waste heat, building design, and city-scale cooling infrastructure.

The second category is water-cycle restoration.

This includes rainwater retention, infiltration, groundwater recharge, wetland recovery, river restoration, evapotranspiration recovery, and reduced runoff.

Water-cycle restoration is central because water is the Earth’s natural heat-transfer system.

The third category is soil regeneration.

This includes increased organic matter, improved soil moisture, restored microorganisms, reduced erosion, improved soil structure, and stronger plant resilience.

Healthy soil supports both carbon storage and cooling.

The fourth category is forest and vegetation cooling.

This includes transpiration, shade, biodiversity, canopy recovery, fire resilience, watershed protection, and functional ecosystem restoration.

The goal is not simply to plant trees.

The goal is to restore forests as living cooling systems.

The fifth category is ocean and coastal function.

This includes improved dissolved oxygen, reduced dead zones, healthier nutrient balance, coastal ecosystem recovery, marine biodiversity, phytoplankton support, and careful circulation support where appropriate.

The ocean is the largest heat reservoir on Earth.

Its function cannot be excluded from climate evaluation.

The sixth category is direct cooling.

This includes responsible interventions that reduce heat load directly, such as urban cooling systems, water misting, surface cooling, heat-reflective design, and carefully monitored ocean or land-based cooling support.

Direct cooling must always be treated carefully.

It must not replace emission reduction or nature restoration.

It must act as a supporting measure.

The seventh category is resilience.

Cooling is not only about temperature.

It is also about whether systems can survive heat.

A project that improves drought resilience, reduces fire risk, stabilizes water supply, protects ecosystems, or lowers heat-related health risk may contribute to cooling resilience.

This broader view is necessary.

Climate action should not only reduce numbers.

It should keep living systems alive.

MRV: Measurement, Reporting, and Verification

Cooling Credits require MRV.

MRV means measurement, reporting, and verification.

Without MRV, credits become empty claims.

A project cannot simply say:

We cooled the Earth.

It must show evidence.

Measurement means collecting data.

This may include temperature data, soil moisture, vegetation cover, evapotranspiration, water retention, carbon storage, biodiversity indicators, oxygen levels, nutrient balance, and heat-load changes.

Reporting means making the data transparent.

The project must explain what was measured, how it was measured, where it was measured, and what changed.

Verification means that the claims must be checked.

This may involve independent review, satellite data, field measurements, sensors, local monitoring, scientific standards, and public documentation.

Cooling Credits must avoid the mistakes of weak carbon credits.

They must not be based only on promises.
They must not rely on vague claims.
They must not double count.
They must not overestimate.
They must not ignore ecological risk.
They must not ignore local people.
They must not reward projects that would have happened anyway.

Strong MRV is essential.

Otherwise, Cooling Credits could become another accounting illusion.

It may appear as stronger ecosystem resilience.

They vary by season.

They may require different methods.

This complexity is a challenge.

A Cooling Credit framework should not pretend that all cooling can be reduced to one perfect number.

It can evaluate cooling contribution by category.

For example:

Urban cooling may use surface temperature, air temperature, shade area, energy demand reduction, and water retention.

Soil cooling may use soil organic matter, soil moisture, infiltration, vegetation cover, and surface temperature.

Forest cooling may use evapotranspiration, canopy cover, biodiversity, soil moisture, carbon fixation, and fire resilience.

Ocean cooling and circulation support may use surface temperature, dissolved oxygen, nutrient balance, phytoplankton response, harmful bloom risk, and ecosystem indicators.

Different projects need different metrics.

The goal is not artificial simplicity.

The goal is honest evaluation.

Additionality

A Cooling Credit must consider additionality.

Additionality means that the cooling benefit would not have happened without the project or intervention.

If a forest was already protected, simply claiming credits for its existing cooling function may not be enough.

If a city was already planning a cooling project, the credit must identify what additional effect the credit created.

If a soil project would have happened anyway, the claim must be limited.

Additionality is important because credits are meant to encourage new or strengthened action.

Without additionality, credits can reward business as usual.

This was one of the problems in some carbon-credit systems.

Cooling Credits must be careful from the beginning.

They should reward real additional cooling contribution.

Not imaginary contribution.
Not already-existing benefit.
Not recycled claims.
Not accounting tricks.

Permanence

Permanence is also important.

Some cooling effects may be temporary.

A newly planted forest may die from drought or fire.
A soil restoration project may reverse if management stops.
A wetland may be drained later.
An urban cooling project may be removed.
An ocean intervention may produce only short-term effects.

A Cooling Credit framework must recognize duration.

A temporary cooling effect is not worthless.

But it should not be treated the same as a long-term recovery.

The system should distinguish between short-term cooling, seasonal cooling, long-term ecosystem recovery, and permanent infrastructure change.

For example, emergency cooling during a heat wave may save lives.
That is valuable.

But it is different from restoring a forest watershed that cools and stabilizes a region over decades.

Both may matter.

But they should be evaluated differently.

Leakage

Leakage means that benefits in one place may cause damage elsewhere.

For example, a forest restoration project may protect one area but push deforestation into another area.

A water-retention project may improve one region but reduce downstream flow if poorly designed.

A cooling intervention may reduce heat locally but increase energy demand elsewhere.

A biomass project may claim carbon or cooling benefit while causing land-use damage in another region.

Cooling Credits must avoid leakage.

A project should not move the problem.

It should reduce the problem.

This requires system-level thinking.

Because cooling is connected to water, land, energy, and ecology, projects must be evaluated beyond narrow boundaries.

Double counting occurs when the same benefit is claimed more than once.

For example, a forest project might claim carbon credits and Cooling Credits for the same function without clear separation.

This does not mean a project cannot have both carbon and cooling benefits.

It can.

But the accounting must be transparent.

Carbon storage and cooling contribution are related but not identical.

A project may receive recognition for carbon storage under one system and cooling contribution under another, but the claims must be clearly defined.

The same outcome should not be sold or counted twice as if it were separate.

Transparency is essential.

Cooling Credits must be designed to avoid confusion and inflation.

The third pillar is direct cooling.

Direct cooling means intentional actions that reduce heat load.

This may include urban cooling, water-based cooling, reflective surfaces, shade structures, heat-reduction materials, mist systems, restored urban water circulation, reduction of waste heat, and carefully monitored support for ocean or land-based cooling where appropriate.

Energy use must become cleaner and more efficient.

But CO₂ is also a result.

It is a result of the weakening of the Earth’s natural systems that once absorbed, fixed, stored, circulated, and buffered carbon.

Forests that once fixed carbon and cooled land through transpiration have been cut, degraded, simplified, and burned.

Soils that once stored organic matter, held water, supported microorganisms, and sustained vegetation have been weakened.

Water cycles that once moved heat through evaporation, clouds, rainfall, rivers, groundwater, wetlands, and oceans have been disrupted.

Organic matter cycles that once returned biological material to soil have been broken by linear food and waste systems.

Oceans that once absorbed heat and carbon through living circulation systems are under pressure from warming, stratification, acidification, oxygen decline, nutrient imbalance, and ecosystem degradation.

Cities that could have been designed around water, shade, vegetation, and cooling have often become heat-retaining structures made of asphalt, concrete, glass, steel, waste heat, and sealed ground.

In this sense, global warming is not only the result of adding too much CO₂ to the atmosphere.

It is also the result of damaging the planetary systems that once kept carbon and heat moving through living cycles.

This is the deeper causal structure.

Humanity increased the inflow of heat and carbon.
At the same time, humanity weakened the cooling system.

It pressed the accelerator.
And it damaged the brakes, the radiator, and the circulation system.

This is why emission reduction alone is not enough.

It is necessary, but not sufficient.

To address global warming, humanity must reduce emissions and restore the Earth’s natural cooling functions at the same time.

This means restoring forests as cooling organs, not only carbon sinks.

It means regenerating soils as living carbon and water reservoirs.

It means returning organic matter to land instead of treating it only as waste.

It means rebuilding water cycles through wetlands, rivers, groundwater, rainwater retention, vegetation, and soil moisture.

It means redesigning cities as cooling structures rather than heat traps.

It means protecting and restoring ocean circulation, oxygen systems, phytoplankton, coastal ecosystems, and land-to-sea nutrient balance.

It means using direct cooling only as a responsible support measure where heat risk has already become dangerous.

It means measuring not only carbon reduction, but real cooling contribution.

This is why the concept of Cooling Credits matters.

Cooling Credits are not financial permission slips.
They are not excuses for continued emissions.
They are not replacements for emission reduction.

They are a proposed framework for evaluating actions that restore cooling capacity, reduce heat load, regenerate natural systems, and support the Earth’s ability to cool itself.

Carbon credits ask:

How much CO₂ was reduced, avoided, removed, or offset?

Cooling Credits ask:

How much cooling function was restored?
How much heat load was reduced?
How much water circulation recovered?
How much soil function returned?
How much forest transpiration improved?
How much ocean oxygen and circulation were supported?
How much resilience was rebuilt?

Both questions matter.

But the second question has been undervalued.

The world has spent years building carbon accounting systems.

Now it must build cooling evaluation systems.

A climate policy that counts carbon but ignores cooling remains incomplete.

A climate policy that reduces emissions but leaves forests, soils, water cycles, oceans, and cities damaged cannot fully restore planetary stability.

The next climate framework must expand.

From carbon to circulation.
From offsetting to restoration.
From accounting to cooling.
From extraction to regeneration.
From heat-retaining civilization to cooling civilization.

The purpose is not to control the Earth like a machine.

The purpose is to stop damaging the systems that already allow the Earth to regulate itself.

The purpose is to restore the conditions under which forests breathe water into the atmosphere, soils hold life and moisture, rivers connect land and sea, oceans circulate heat and carbon, cities release heat instead of storing it, and civilization operates within planetary circulation rather than against it.

This is not a rejection of science.

It is an expansion of the frame.

CO₂ matters.
Greenhouse gases matter.
Emission reduction matters.

But forests also matter.
Soils matter.
Water matters.
Oceans matter.
Microorganisms matter.
Organic matter matters.
Transpiration matters.
Phytoplankton matters.
Urban heat matters.
Dead zones matter.
Natural cooling functions matter.

Global warming is not a single-variable problem.

It is a planetary circulation problem.

Therefore, the solution must be a planetary circulation solution.

The next decade must not become another lost decade.

It must not be a decade of carbon accounting without cooling recovery.

It must not be a decade of credits without restoration.

It must not be a decade of targets without planetary repair.

It must become a decade of rebuilding the Earth’s ability to cool itself.

That requires three pillars:

Emission reduction.
Nature restoration.
Direct cooling.

Emission reduction slows the acceleration.

Nature restoration repairs the Earth’s natural cooling systems.

Direct cooling provides careful support where heat risk is already dangerous.

Together, these three pillars form the practical structure of a complete climate response.

The question for the future is not only:

How much carbon did humanity reduce?

Can humanity restore the Earth as a planet capable of cooling itself again?
